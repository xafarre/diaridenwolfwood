The ink must still be wet on the papers that opened the prison door for us when
the order comes to empty the block. People begin to move and we advance in the
same white gowns, without luggage and with no proof of freedom but the contract
we have just signed. Alessa is waiting on the other side of the door.

She must be about twenty-five. She is short and slim, with a lively beauty that
her impatient expression makes difficult to mistake for fragility. Her blonde
hair is tied into two ponytails and her lips are painted red. Her white shirt,
profusely decorated at the collar and cuffs, is cinched by a corset above a
short skirt. The cut recalls a servant’s uniform, but the quality of the fabric
and the explosive confidence with which she examines us do not suggest someone
accustomed to obeying. Kotor, her assistant, stands beside her, discreet enough
that she commands all the attention.

—You are the three who chose Suelex. Not a bad start. Come along, we have to
reach the station. If you want to know anything, ask while we walk, but do not
fall behind.

Alessa is already walking as she speaks. We have to follow her through the
people leaving the block, and I use the journey to draw closer to her.

—Is anyone already settled in Suelex?

—Not yet. We are still investigating what happened to the previous expedition.

—Then will the train take us straight there?

—No. You will wait at an intermediate point until we close the investigation.

She stops neither to deliver the news nor to see how we receive it.

The station appears before we can press her. About twenty platforms stretch
beneath its great iron arches. The locomotives expel enough smoke to make an
entire section of the concourse vanish, only to reveal it again moments later.
Following Alessa is easier than trying to find our way among so many tracks.

Our train stands out for its finer finish. The line must be close to fifty
carriages long, and workers are still moving up and down it making preparations.
We follow its side past windows and doors until we reach a sheet-metal carriage
with blank walls, quite unlike the others. They take us aboard to receive the
first dose of the treatment.

  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/diary/acte-2-estacio-moceria-v13.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Elevated view of Moceria’s monumental terminal, with four steam trains, an upper railway line, a clock tower, a moored zeppelin and vast plumes of smoke." loading="lazy">

Inside is a narrow clinic mounted on wheels. The person receiving us wears a
white coat, a leather mask with two filters and sealed goggles. We cannot see a
single finger’s breadth of skin. Gebedayah goes first and has to turn his
shoulders to pass through a doorway that seems to have been designed without
imagining a body like his.

The attendant produces a metal cylinder and connects one end of the tube to the
machine. Pale condensation beads on the metal. He closes a valve, finds a vein
in Gebedayah’s enormous arm and drives in the needle. I can follow the procedure
with a doctor’s eye; knowing who made that dose or through whose hands it passed
is another matter. Khor and I receive the same treatment. We have seen how they
administer it, but the person doing so still has not shown us an inch of skin.

When we finish, they take us to a much more elegant carriage. Tables extend
from beneath the tall, narrow windows, each set between two facing benches. Ours
is meant for four, although Gebedayah occupies an entire bench merely by sitting
down. Khor and I share the one opposite. At the far end, beside the door, a man
sits on a single chair facing into the carriage.

His name is Obaric. He wears a close-fitting security uniform fastened by a row
of buttons along one side, and a police-style cap. Metal guards on his shoulders,
forearms and insteps complete his equipment. His skin is another matter: hard,
chitin-like plates trace his temples and jaw, then continue down his neck. His
shins are short and his heels raised; his broad, custom-made boots follow the
shape of his feet without concealing the mutation. A long wooden staff is
within reach, and a mask with lenses hangs from his uniform.

Before we can speak to him, Coti, a journalist from the *Torrevijía* newspaper,
asks to interview us individually. When my turn comes, she opens her notebook
and fires off questions without giving me time to decide which deserves an
answer.

—Where are you from? What were you doing at the casino? Why did you choose
Suelex?

She raises her pencil before I can reply.

—Is someone waiting for you there? Do you want to send any message before you
leave?

Her eccentricity seems too attentive to the effect of each question to be
entirely spontaneous. I have the impression that she has already decided what
kind of tragedy she needs and lacks only our words to give it a human face. I
may be wrong, but I try not to give her more than she asks.

Khor heads towards Alessa, who is sitting at the far end of the carriage. When
he passes, Obaric advises him to leave her alone, but Khor does not slow down.

—If you want them to make the journey miserable, go ahead —Obaric insists—. If
you would prefer a little peace, stay here.

—You can advise me against it all you like. I am going.

—Before you do: what is your name?

Khor does not answer. Obaric opens his hand and shows him a sweet.

—Not even this?

Khor walks past. I pause beside Obaric long enough to accept the sweet before
following him. We do not manage to start a conversation: as we approach, Alessa
stands and leaves the carriage without attending to us.

Passengers in the same uniform gradually file in. They do not move like
security personnel or carry Obaric’s equipment; they look like recruits, and
enough of them arrive to fill the carriage. Only then does Alessa return. She
remains near the door, where we can all hear her.

—We have fourteen days of travel ahead. I do not intend to spend them solving
your problems. Anyone who causes trouble will be left outside the train; do not
expect me to wait for a station before putting them off.

She leaves again and the armoured door closes behind her. Shortly afterwards,
the train begins to move.

The track climbs onto an elevated bridge and Moceria opens beyond the glass.
First come the great industrial sheds, freight yards and chimneys discharging
black smoke between columns of white steam. Beyond them, neighbourhoods fill
the valley and climb the dry slopes until they lose definition in the
industrial haze. A city of half a million people, yet it is still difficult to
find where it ends. The bridge lets us see its scale for a few minutes; then
Moceria falls behind.

Food arrives on a trolley pushed by a robot through a second hatch made exactly
to its dimensions. The large doors remain closed. Someone has worked out how to
feed us without letting us out, and soon the meals establish a routine in which
one day is difficult to distinguish from the next. Beyond the glass, by
contrast, the desert gives way to fields of flowers and green expanses. We
remain at the same tables, learning to live with the strangers seated around us.

I speak with Obaric during the journey, once the repetition of the days makes
approaching his chair feel less strange.

—Have you made the journey to Suelex before?

—No. This is the first time.

—And they hired you only to watch us?

—For security —he corrects me—. That you need watching is your conclusion.
Although it does not seem a particularly reckless one.

He says it with the self-assurance that lets him pass judgement on others as
though he had already resolved all their shortcomings. His cynicism does not
prevent him from sustaining a conversation; rather, it seems to be his way of
doing so.

Khor takes longer to return. When he finally approaches the chair by the door,
he does so without the challenge of the first day.

—I did not introduce myself properly before. My name is Khor. Shall we start
again?

Obaric talks with him. The visits to the chair at the rear are repeated and, as
the days pass, Khor seems to be growing on him. They still know little about
each other, but no longer need to take each other’s measure whenever they speak.

During one of these conversations, we ask him about Alessa.

—Are you and Alessa friends? —I ask—. How long have you known her?

—Ali? Do not make the relationship older than it is. I have known her only a
short while; she hired me for security.

—And had she ever been to Suelex?

—She has never been. It is her first assignment too.

The familiarity of the nickname had suggested a longer acquaintance than he
then describes. I retain the answer as I look at the door through which Alessa
disappeared: the confidence with which she gives us orders does not come from
having made this journey before.

The other passengers also begin to define themselves through conversation.
Ogelec’s name passes among the tables as that of the man who hired us. The
company is called Crediant, written in modest serif lettering without much
further ornament. Names travel from group to group; explanations are less
plentiful.

Khor leans towards one of the recruits at the next table.

—What do you plan to do when we reach Suelex?

—We are all going to do the same thing. Or did you not read the contract?

Khor takes too long to answer.

—Your contract and mine need not necessarily be the same.

The other man does not buy it. He looks at Khor as though he has just confirmed
that the young man is a fool, and the disappointment with which he abandons the
conversation seems deeper than a mere difference in paperwork deserves.

We also end up talking about what we were before the prison put us in identical
gowns.

—Are you from Baustra? —I ask Khor.

—No. I travel from one city to another; I have crossed many.

Gebedayah listens in silence until the conversation turns to the places where
we learned to defend ourselves.

—They arrange fights in the factory basements —he explains—. More often than
you might think.

—Did you go down there too? —Khor asks.

—Only once. And I came out the victor.

He says it with the same ease another man might use to discuss shifts. The story
fits all too well with the number of blows his body seems able to endure.

—What about you, Nicholas? —Khor asks—. Is no one waiting for you?

—There is no room for love. I have already consecrated my life to Electra.

It is a truth clear enough to end the conversation and incomplete enough to
protect everything I do not intend to explain in a carriage full of strangers.

We have been away from Moceria for about ten days when we stop in a village in
an arid region. The station is small and open to the sky, with two tracks and a
single platform punished by dust. Lorries approach with cargo and workers
distribute it along the train, much of which stretches beyond the station’s
limits. It seems as though all the village’s activity has been concentrated on
supplying our train.

—You have two hours to get off and stretch your legs —Obaric tells us—. Go and
find Alessa before you lose them both.

We make our way towards the front carriages, where we see passengers in
well-tailored clothes of a quality that suggests money. Our gowns stand out
even more there than they did among the recruits’ uniforms.

When we find her, Alessa takes us to the rear of the train. In what appears to
be a goods carriage, she gives us the same clothes as the rest of the
contingent: shirt, braces, trousers, boots and a zippered jacket. We have spent
these ten days in our prison gowns. Dressing does not restore our confiscated
possessions, but at least we no longer stand out among the other recruits.

Outside, the loading continues. The village is small and we have neither money
nor possessions with which to occupy ourselves; after we have walked for a
while, there is little left to do. We return to the carriage before the two
hours expire and resume our journey.

Beyond the window, the pillars of a Tesla train under construction appear. The
great U-shaped structures repeat across the land, tracing the corridor through
which the magnetic train will travel. The works remain incomplete, but
electrical arcs are already visible: brief discharges outlining the structure.
Seen from our steam carriage, that succession of supports looks excessive even
for a train.

Obaric leans towards the glass.

—When they finish it, a magnetic train will pass through those structures.
Large, fast and without dragging itself along a track like ours. I would board
it now if they let us.

—I will believe it when I see one pass —Khor replies.

—I would board it with you —I tell Obaric—. We Custodians of Electra would be
hard pressed to find a more fitting work of transport: all that weight held by
an invisible force is also a way of giving form to what we worship.

For a while we talk about the train we would like to take instead of the one
carrying us.

Farther on, to the left, the landscape loses every familiar shape.

Cártigo is the former capital, but from the window I see no city. I see an
immense, dark grey expanse, with contours that seem to change as the train
moves. Frightening stories describe it as a land cursed by the void, from which
abominations emerge; religion teaches that terrible things have occurred
wherever the void is found. I recognise the stories, not what lies before my
eyes. I search for some stable form in that darkness, and the train’s movement
unravels it before I can decide what I have seen.

We leave the shifting stain behind and return to increasingly arid land. Four
days after the stop in the village, the train slows before the tents, barracks
and vehicles of Ballard. The name belongs to a military camp, not the town of
Suelex promised in our contract.

The brakes screech. The armoured door opens for the first time since the stop,
admitting harsh light, dust and voices of command. Obaric is already outside
beside the step, staff in hand and mask hanging from his uniform. He looks at us
as though the days on the train had been no more than a waiting room.

<figure class="diary-illustration">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/diary/acte-2-arribada-ballard-v5.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Gebedayah, colossal on the same ground line as Obaric, has already stepped down from the long train at Ballard; Khor and Nicholas remain on the steps while Obaric orders them towards the arid military camp." loading="lazy">
</figure>

—The journey is over, for now! Everyone out of the carriage!

Gebedayah stoops to pass through the door. Khor climbs down behind him. I set
foot in Ballard without knowing what they have prepared for us, but with a new
certainty: the Suelex contract does not begin in the town. It has been
classifying us for days already.
