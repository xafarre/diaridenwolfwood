In prison they do not treat us like criminals, but nor do they ask our
permission.

They take us into a room and begin classifying us according to what our papers
and bodies reveal. Three frightened men are with us, all so unremarkable that I
already know I will not remember any of them. The other two are Khor Yugoroto
and Gebedayah Brass. I know nothing about them yet; I confine myself to what I
can observe while the guards review records and ask questions.

Khor is a black panther standing on two legs, roughly my height. He has yellow
eyes, a scar across one eye and a gaze that shifts from the police to us without
revealing whom he considers more dangerous. He has no tail. He is dressed as a
nobleman and, at first glance, almost looks the part. A closer inspection,
however, reveals small inconsistencies in the tailoring and combination of the
garments. I do not know whether he is trying to deceive someone or simply
dresses according to his idea of the aristocracy.

Gebedayah stands two metres sixty tall and makes the room feel smaller. His
papers identify him as a second-generation OG-207. He has enormous shoulders,
prominent muscles and coarse skin punished by scars and burns. When he tells the
guards that he works in a metalworks, the marks on his body make the trade
credible: they look like the result of years among furnaces and loads that
would force other men to find a machine.

My record holds their attention longer. They hesitate, read it again and, when
they make me uncover my left hand, find it even harder to accept that I am a
priest. I tell them it is a modification designed for healing, but they demand
a demonstration.

Khor has an open wound in his side, perhaps sustained during the unrest. I
press my palm against it and activate the mechanism. I administer no local
anaesthetic. The four metacarpals rotate until they stand perpendicular and
pass through ports in the knuckles; only one centimetre of each remains visible
on the back of my hand. Electrical arcs leap between the four terminals. Under
my palm, the rods penetrate the tissue while the mechanism hums and smoke
escapes along the sides and between my fingers. The smell of burnt flesh fills
the room, but Khor keeps his composure. When I remove my hand, the wound has
been cauterised. The demonstration convinces them or, at the very least, gives
them a box in which to place me. They stop asking questions.

Then they force us to undress.

They wash us without consideration, as though we were not people but parts
removed from a contaminated room. The water falls so cold that it hurts before
the body can react. I assume the cold is meant to solidify any traces of Zolio
still on us, but no one explains it. When it ends, my skin burns and my teeth
chatter. Gebedayah endures it almost without moving; Khor keeps his eyes fixed
on the men in uniform.

Our clothes and everything we carry go into containers marked for incineration.
No one protests until an officer picks up a small figurine belonging to Khor.
He asks them not to burn it, without raising his voice but firmly enough to make
the police confer. They promise him nothing. Instead of throwing it in with the
rest, one of the officers seals it in another bag and takes it away separately,
as though they mean to isolate it while checking whether it emits any residue.
It is no guarantee, but at least they do not consign it to the fire in front of
him.

They save nothing else. After the cleansing, they give each of us a white
prison gown and lock us in the block they have emptied for quarantine.

We will spend three nights there. That, they tell us, is how long we need to
cease being contagious. During the days we do little more than sleep, eat and
talk.

On that same first day we visit the office of the policeman responsible for the
paperwork and for recording where each of us will be sent when quarantine ends.
He presents us with two options. The first is Contart, a poor district with a
hospital large enough to administer the partial treatment every day. It will
not cure us, but will keep the disease under control for as long as we have
access to it.

The other option is Suelex.

The policeman spends more time on it than on Contart, though not to recommend
it. He says it is an almost deserted mining town, fifteen or twenty days away
by train, near the border with a country with which ours does not enjoy good
relations. The region answers to a nobleman who wants to reopen a Zolio mine,
one of the richest remaining, and repopulate the town with workers, services
and police. He has tried before. The plans fail and volunteers are scarce. The
policeman does not speak of accidents or bodies. He speaks of disappearances.

When something happens in Suelex, he continues, the State is slow to arrive, if
it goes at all. And when it finally sends someone, they often find nothing that
explains what happened. The policeman cannot give us a better answer or does not
want to. Either way, he makes sure we understand that Suelex is not a
destination he would choose.

Without streets, work or possessions, time is reduced to the arrival of trays,
the changing of the guards and a choice that grows more complicated the harder
we try to understand it. Every day we return to the office to clear up our
doubts, but each answer opens a new question. We ask who prepares the treatment,
how it is preserved on such a long journey, whether it arrives by train or
airship and who will administer it. He does not know or will not tell us.

Khor listens like someone searching for a crack and asks chiefly about Suelex.
The firmness with which he defended the figurine and the persistence with which
he returns to the town make me think of someone accustomed to surviving without
losing sight of what he seeks. Gebedayah needs fewer words. His hearing, damaged
by years in factories, sometimes forces him to tilt his head or ask for a
sentence to be repeated, but he waits with the patience of someone who has
always lived under a whistle that decides when the day begins and ends.

I return again and again to the treatment. If it must reach an almost abandoned
town, it must come from somewhere and pass through hands that we may be able to
identify.

As the end of quarantine approaches, Khor has already made his choice: he wants
to go to Suelex. Gebedayah prefers Contart; for him, the hospital and guaranteed
treatment carry more weight than a distant mine surrounded by disappearances.
Even so, he does not try to impose his will. He says he will accept whatever the
group decides, as though a destination were another factory shift: perhaps
worse than the last, but just as inevitable.

I understand Contart’s appeal. The treatment is there, inside a hospital, and
does not depend on a promise signed by a nobleman we cannot see, who has not
even sent a subordinate to explain the undertaking. I can also imagine what
would follow: whatever work I could find in a poor district, days arranged
around the hospital, and the next dose deciding how far I can travel. The
partial treatment halts the decline while I receive it, but does not eliminate
the disease. Contart would keep me alive as long as no administration failed
and no one decided to withdraw it. It is a form of safety that feels too much
like awaiting a slow death without being able to pursue anything else.

Suelex’s promise is far more fragile, but its deficiencies leave a trail. The
treatment cannot appear in the town at the nobleman’s command: someone must
manufacture, transport and preserve it before other hands administer it to us.
Every step may reveal a name, a route or a place. In Contart, the hospital would
show us only the final link; in Suelex, where they are still trying to build the
operation that must sustain the town, perhaps we can see the chain. If I can
trace it backwards, I might reach those who control the treatment and, through
them, a complete cure.

The thought carries me further still. A stolen shipment could buy information
or money. A zeppelin loaded with Zolio, in hands willing to turn it into a
threat, might force those who control the cure to negotiate. It is not a plan.
I do not yet know who transports the Zolio, who protects it or whom any action
might endanger. But in Contart I would not even have the chance to learn.

On the third night we discuss it one last time. Khor will not be moved from
Suelex. Gebedayah has not changed his mind either, but he keeps his word. I am
no longer comparing a safe place with a dangerous one, but a dependence without
an escape with a risk that may teach me how to break it. I need a complete cure,
not a daily reprieve, and I am willing to go looking for it. I choose Suelex.

When the moment comes, we return to the office. The policeman goes over the
terms once more and leaves us room to sign. All three of us do.

When he collects the papers, we are not yet companions. But we already share a
destination.
