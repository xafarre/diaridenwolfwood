My first step off the train leaves me facing an expanse of iron I cannot take in at a glance. On either side of the open ground stand artillery machines as tall as houses, mounted on wheels and tracks capable of sinking into the dry earth. Their guns cast long shadows across the barracks. Robots of different shapes move among their legs and frames, some carrying crates, others dragging ammunition or tools. Beyond them, enormous tractors shift entire platforms and make the ground tremble beneath our boots.

Ballard is not a town built around a station. It is a base that looks prepared to move a war.

Obaric drives us forward with the other passengers. There are nearly two hundred newcomers, all wearing the uniforms issued during the journey. Most of us are humans without visible modifications; we could pass for workers, unarmed soldiers, or people who signed contracts without yet knowing what would be demanded of them.

They gather us before a platform. Alessa climbs it, watches us fill the open ground, and withdraws to one side. She introduces no one. She simply yields the centre to the men who will decide what to do with us.

Náster, the security chief, steps forward first. His suit is strangely elegant for the role, finer than the guards’ uniforms and topped with a hat like those worn by many of the officials. A broad metal collar rises to his nose. Cables emerge from the back and curve towards his shoulders and arms. I cannot tell what part of him they connect.

Náster waits for the murmuring to die.

“You signed up to reclaim Suelex. Anyone who causes trouble will be eliminated.”

He does not raise his voice, as though stating a condition made sufficiently clear in the contract.

“While the Council determines what happened to the earlier expedition, we will prepare the selected recruits. When the time comes, you will go directly to Suelex. This time will be a success. If there are conflicts, speak to me.”

He steps back and makes room for Mánefer. The maintenance chief has a bionic eye and two broad mechanical arms, with pistons and joints visible below his short sleeves. His work waistcoat is well made, but his unkempt hair and goatee remove any air of ceremony. His trousers conceal another rigid shape I cannot identify. Perhaps the modifications continue into his legs; from where I stand, I can only tell that something is there.

“I want the equipment ready,” he says.

He adds nothing. In his mouth, equipment might mean the machines, us, or both.

Sazón, the mines chief, comes next. He must be about thirty: pale, red-haired, flat-nosed, with a closed expression that offers little reason to imagine patience. He takes the centre of the platform and studies us before speaking.

“In the mines I need efficiency and speed. You are the future of this operation. We will extract dangerous materials, and that work is not for everyone.”

He lets the words settle among the recruits.

“I will be hard, but I will also be a comrade. You know the danger. We need hard people. There will be no softness. I can be kind, but do not test me.”

The promise of kindness does not alter the face with which he delivers it.

“We have failed before. Ogelec, the gentleman, wants an outright success. Our benefactor expects much from you. On other occasions there have been losses through poor handling. It will not happen again.”

I do not know who that benefactor is or why his expectations weigh more than the dead from the earlier expeditions. No one interrupts Sazón to ask.

Sísaro takes his place when the mines chief withdraws. He is responsible for logistics: a beard, an unfriendly face, and no visible prosthesis except at his right eye, where something like leather holds a lens. He gives no speech that I can retain. He watches us in silence, as though his work has already begun and we are cargo to be distributed.

When the introductions end, Wúsir climbs the platform. He is about two metres tall and dressed entirely in white. A pointed mask, black goggles, and a white top hat erase almost every trace of expression.

“Go with your supervisor.”

The order breaks the formation. Obaric steps forward so we can follow him and draws us away from the flow of other recruits. We walk among manoeuvres, military trials, and commands arriving from different directions. The noise of motors, metal, and gunfire does not stop even when we reach our tent, a canvas barrack with lockers and assigned clothing.

“Every day at eight, the medical carriage,” Obaric tells us. “First thing: showers and washing. Until then, no noise, no laughter, no fights.”

He takes us there before allowing us to rest. The carriage remains a foreign body within the camp, a clinic mounted on rails. This time a large man receives us with nervous friendliness, too quick to feel reassuring.

“Ah, a pleasure. Is it you? The blue-Zolio cases?”

He studies our faces while smiling.

“Do not worry. The rest do not know who you are.”

“What do you do?” Khor asks.

“I lead the medical team.”

The answer gives me a more important question.

“Have you treated other Zolio cases?”

“None. That is what makes it interesting.”

There is something admirable in his honesty and very little that is comforting. We return to the barracks knowing that, to the man responsible for keeping us alive, we too are a novelty worth studying.

The following morning they wake us to wash and dress in uniform. Obaric is waiting outside.

“At the double!”

He has us running before our bodies have finished understanding that the day has begun. We reach a slope, and when our pace falls, his voice pursues us from behind.

“Do not slow down!”

We fall a little behind. I try to push harder, but my legs are already giving everything they have. The ground levels out and we recover some breath before the first wall. We have to climb it. I struggle to find holds and reach the top, but I make it. The second goes better. Then we run again until the path opens before a lake.

“Everyone swims,” Obaric orders.

Gebedayah and Khor stop. In both I recognize something deeper than exhaustion: they fear deep water. Obaric insists. When neither moves, the threat ceases to be implicit.

“Get in or you will be eliminated.”

They hesitate long enough for the silence to weigh more than the shouting. Gebedayah jumps first. His enormous body strikes the water with enough force to seem capable of emptying half the lake, but he moves with unexpected competence. It is not elegant; it is enough.

Khor holds out longer. Obaric repeats the order with less patience and greater anger. When Khor finally jumps, black water covers him, and a moment later we see him moving with astonishing speed. Obaric runs along the shore to keep pace with us. He watches Khor as though the relief of discovering that ability cannot compensate for having had to tear it out of him.

We continue running to camp. The morning training ends in a barrack resembling a tactical classroom: rows of tables and chairs facing a platform and blackboard. Khor still wears his refusal in every line of his body.

“I am not swimming again.”

Obaric hears him.

“You will do it every day. The Zolio mines are under water.”

The news turns the lake into more than a test. It also explains why Sazón placed such emphasis on toughness and why no preference written on paper can be innocent.

The officers hand us precisely such a paper. We must rank four destinations: mines, logistics, maintenance, and security. I put logistics first, security second, mines third, and maintenance fourth. I return the sheet without knowing whether they asked what we want or merely wish to compare desire with obedience.

Next comes a questionnaire. I know very little, but my answers are better than they should be. The luck continues at the firing range. When they hand me a pistol, I pretend not to understand its weight or the proper stance. I fire and hit.

“Lucky me,” I say, with a laugh empty enough to serve as an excuse.

I hit with the Spencer carbine too. Khor is less fortunate: he aims, fires, and almost sends the shot towards people in the range area. An officer pulls him aside immediately with a shout.

The next test takes us to open ground. They divide us into randomly chosen pairs and make us fight. My opponent gives me no time to feign incompetence; I hit him and do enough damage for the contest to end with a clear conclusion.

Then come the maintenance tests. I repair what they put before me well, perhaps better than expected. With electronics, however, the luck runs out. The circuits resist me and leave a far less flattering result.

Obaric considers the whole as though the tests have produced an answer that does not quite fit us.

“I do not know where you came from.”

I cannot tell whether it is disappointment or surprise. I still cannot read him.

“You used to be a doctor, did you not?” he asks.

I tell him I did.

“We will continue testing you to determine where you go. I will take your file into account.”

Ballard is not a stop before Suelex. It is where they take us apart into pieces: the part that endures water, the part that knows how to repair, the part that aims, the part that obeys. When they are finished, they will decide which part of us is useful and where they intend to spend it.
