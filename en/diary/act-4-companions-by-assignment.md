Two days before departure, we are still at the base when we ask Obanic whether there is anywhere to get a drink. There is no bar in camp, but he knows one in a nearby town. We invite him along.

“Suppose I have to. Besides, I am no longer your supervisor. We are colleagues now.”

“Perhaps we will get along better,” Khor says.

“I am hoping to see the back of you.”

The reply is curt enough to sound sincere, but I am beginning to suspect Obanic needs to preserve that manner more than the distance it creates. He does agree to come with us, after all.

We leave after lunch, around three, on an enormous bus that moves slowly and trails smoke outside. Inside, we are packed together. When we arrive, the main avenue, wooden buildings and shops give the town a frontier air. There are more people and more activity than I expected this close to camp.

We go straight into the bar. Obanic gives the waiter the company number: our food and non-alcoholic drinks are covered. They serve us aromatic spiced water, rather like very weak tea. Beer is outside the arrangement.

I get up as though heading for the lavatory and approach the man who seems to run the place.

“Do you know where I could find vigilia?”

He does not seem to understand. I cannot tell whether he is unfamiliar with it or whether I have asked the wrong person, but I give him no time to make me clarify.

“A newspaper. I would like to know what is happening back home.”

“The newspaper is the Torrevijía.”

He gives me a copy. I take it away without learning anything about what I was looking for; at least the question now has an answer I can show someone.

Meanwhile, Khor is talking to a short, plump waitress with loose, slightly greasy hair. I do not find her particularly attractive. Her clothes are clean, with damp patches from washing dishes, and her stress comes through in the way she demands them.

“Juan! The plates!”

Khor explains that he had hoped to drink beer, but we have no money and the company will not pay for it. She says there is nothing she can do. He persists: we leave for the mines in two days. After five weeks of tests, he would like a drink before we go.

“I do not like doing this…” she finally relents. “But only one.”

She exchanges his glass for a pint, without charging him and taking care that the boss does not see. Khor returns pleased and gives me roughly half. We have little to celebrate, but after those weeks the toast feels earned.

We talk to Obanic about Suelex again. He assures us everything is ready and confirms that he too is going into security. When we try to pin down the threats, his answers become terse again. Khor’s beer has proved easier to obtain than an explanation.

We ask what he used to do.

“I played aerial hockey. Professionally.”

“What happened?”

“I made a mistake. I cannot do it any more.”

He does not say what mistake. Alessa found him during that collapse; according to him, she looks for people with potential. I do not know what she saw in us, but I am beginning to understand that we are not the only ones who arrived here after losing another life.

Gebedayah concentrates on a generous plate of chicken and potatoes. We return to camp and sleep. Most people have already left; I still have not read the newspaper.

The next morning, Samón receives us. He is an enormous man, though Gebedayah is bigger. He has human skin, prominent tusks and a nose that gives his face a harsh cast; his hair is tied back, with a few strands falling forward.

“Mine group: you are under my command.”

We have been assigned to security. He offers no explanation for the name, and I focus on the actual order: we must form teams of four, with a leader to be added. There are three of us.

We look at the other recruits. Among those remaining I can make out no mutations or implants, though some supervisors have them. We approach the women in the room to look for a fourth member. The first one I speak to already has a team. Khor’s conversation seems to be going better.

Carla is a farmer and lives to the south, near Suelex, where she was recruited. When she talks about hunting, terrain and shooting, they come across as practical skills learnt through use.

“Everyone hunts in my village,” she says.

Khor talks to a former acrobat who is good with a sword. Gebedayah approaches Mònica, who practises martial arts. She is slim and wiry, with a serious expression; her shoulders and arms show strength developed through training. She explains that she could not earn a living from it and accepted the company’s offer.

I bring Carla over to the others, and Gebedayah does the same with Mònica. Both seem like good candidates. We explain that we need one more person and end up asking a question we consider useful.

“Could either of you bring Gebedayah down?”

They do not like it at all. They feel judged and compared, and say so. We try to explain that we want the best fit, that we meant no offence.

“We are forming a work team,” they reply, “not a perfect team. This is no way to go about it.”

Their reactions strike me as remarkably similar, too sensitive for the expedition ahead. Perhaps I expected a more practical answer. Each goes her own way, and there are still three of us.

Carla approaches Toni, who seems to have shared her barracks and offers her a place on his team. He is tall, with slicked-back hair and a long face with a prominent nose. He talks and gestures constantly, even when no one seems to be listening. I find him tiresome before we have exchanged a word.

I approach Carla again to try to resolve the misunderstanding, but Toni intervenes.

“Are you the infected ones?”

I tell him we are. I have no reason to hide it. He asks a few curious questions and explains that he wants to work in security, with a casual attitude towards the place that I find hard to share.

“Do not underestimate zolio,” I warn him.

He turns to Carla.

“Come with me. These people are very boring.”

I leave, but still feel compelled to say, loudly enough for him to hear, that I would not choose such a reckless companion. I return to Khor and Gebedayah, satisfied that I have made my opinion clear.

Mònica eventually comes back. She seems more willing to join us now, and we accept her. Samón asks whether we have finished and introduces our assigned leader.

It is Toni. My solemn assessment is still recent enough that I need not repeat it.
