My first step off the train leaves me facing an expanse of iron I cannot take in at a glance. On either side of the open ground stand artillery machines as tall as houses, mounted on wheels and tracks capable of sinking into the dry earth. Their guns cast long shadows across the barracks. Robots of different shapes move among their legs and frames, some carrying crates, others dragging ammunition or tools. Beyond them, enormous tractors shift entire platforms and make the ground tremble beneath our boots.

Ballard is not a town built around a station. It is a base that looks prepared to move a war.

Obanic drives us forward with the other passengers. There are nearly two hundred newcomers, all wearing the uniforms issued during the journey. Most of us are humans without visible modifications; we could pass for workers, unarmed soldiers, or people who signed contracts without yet knowing what would be demanded of them.

They gather us before a platform. Alessa climbs it, watches us fill the open ground, and withdraws to one side. She introduces no one. She simply yields the centre to the men who will decide what to do with us.

Náster, the security chief, steps forward first. His suit is strangely elegant for the role, finer than the guards’ uniforms and topped with a hat like those worn by many of the officials. A broad metal collar rises to his nose. Cables emerge from the back and curve towards his shoulders and arms. I cannot tell what part of him they connect.

Náster waits for the murmuring to die.

“You signed up to reclaim Suelex. Anyone who causes trouble will be eliminated.”

He does not raise his voice, as though stating a condition made sufficiently clear in the contract.

“While the Council determines what happened to the earlier expedition, we will prepare the selected recruits. When the time comes, you will go directly to Suelex. This time will be a success. If there are conflicts, speak to me.”

He steps back and makes room for Mánefer. The maintenance chief has a bionic eye and two broad mechanical arms, with pistons and joints visible below his short sleeves. His work waistcoat is well made, but his unkempt hair and goatee remove any air of ceremony. His trousers conceal another rigid shape I cannot identify. Perhaps the modifications continue into his legs; from where I stand, I can only tell that something is there.

“I want the equipment ready,” he says.

He adds nothing. In his mouth, equipment might mean the machines, us, or both.

Sazón, the mines chief, comes next. He must be about thirty: pale, red-haired, flat-nosed, with a closed expression that offers little reason to imagine patience. He takes the centre of the platform and studies us before speaking.

“In the mines I need efficiency and speed. You are the future of this operation. We will extract dangerous materials, and that work is not for everyone.”

He lets the words settle among the recruits.

“I will be hard, but I will also be a comrade. You know the danger. We need hard people. There will be no softness. I can be kind, but do not test me.”

The promise of kindness does not alter the face with which he delivers it.

“We have failed before. Ogelec, the gentleman, wants an outright success. Our benefactor expects much from you. On other occasions there have been losses through poor handling. It will not happen again.”

I do not know who that benefactor is or why his expectations weigh more than the dead from the earlier expeditions. No one interrupts Sazón to ask.

Sísaro takes his place when the mines chief withdraws. He is responsible for logistics: a beard, an unfriendly face, and no visible prosthesis except at his right eye, where something like leather holds a lens. He gives no speech that I can retain. He watches us in silence, as though his work has already begun and we are cargo to be distributed.

When the introductions end, Wúsir climbs the platform. He is about two metres tall and dressed entirely in white. A pointed mask, black goggles, and a white top hat erase almost every trace of expression.

“Go with your supervisor.”

The order breaks the formation. Obanic steps forward so we can follow him and draws us away from the flow of other recruits. We walk among manoeuvres, military trials, and commands arriving from different directions. The noise of motors, metal, and gunfire does not stop even when we reach our tent, a canvas barrack with lockers and assigned clothing.

“Every day at eight, the medical carriage,” Obanic tells us. “First thing: showers and washing. Until then, no noise, no laughter, no fights.”

He takes us there before allowing us to rest. The carriage remains a foreign body within the camp, a clinic mounted on rails. This time a large man receives us with nervous friendliness, too quick to feel reassuring.

“Ah, a pleasure. Is it you? The blue-Zolio cases?”

He studies our faces while smiling.

“Do not worry. The rest do not know who you are.”

“What do you do?” Khor asks.

“I lead the medical team.”

The answer gives me a more important question.

“Have you treated other Zolio cases?”

“None. That is what makes it interesting.”

There is something admirable in his honesty and very little that is comforting. We return to the barracks knowing that, to the man responsible for keeping us alive, we too are a novelty worth studying.

The following morning they wake us to wash and dress in uniform. Obanic is waiting outside.

“At the double!”

He has us running before our bodies have finished understanding that the day has begun. We reach a slope, and when our pace falls, his voice pursues us from behind.

“Do not slow down!”

We fall a little behind. I try to push harder, but my legs are already giving everything they have. The ground levels out and we recover some breath before the first wall. We have to climb it. I struggle to find holds and reach the top, but I make it. The second goes better. Then we run again until the path opens before a lake.

“Everyone swims,” Obanic orders.

Gebedayah and Khor stop. In both I recognize something deeper than exhaustion: they fear deep water. Obanic insists. When neither moves, the threat ceases to be implicit.

“Get in or you will be eliminated.”

They hesitate long enough for the silence to weigh more than the shouting. Gebedayah jumps first. His enormous body strikes the water with enough force to seem capable of emptying half the lake. When he straightens up, his feet reach the bottom: he crosses the training pond by walking along its bed, his head above water.

Khor holds out longer. Obanic repeats the order with less patience and greater anger. When Khor finally jumps, black water covers him, and a moment later we see him moving with astonishing speed. Obanic runs along the shore to keep pace with us. He watches Khor as though the relief of discovering that ability cannot compensate for having had to tear it out of him.

We continue running to camp. The morning training ends in a barrack resembling a tactical classroom: rows of tables and chairs facing a platform and blackboard. Khor still wears his refusal in every line of his body.

“I am not swimming again.”

Obanic hears him.

“You will do it every day. The Zolio mines are under water.”

The news turns the lake into more than a test. It also explains why Sazón placed such emphasis on toughness and why no preference written on paper can be innocent.

The officers hand us precisely such a paper. We must rank four destinations: mines, logistics, maintenance, and security. I put logistics first, security second, mines third, and maintenance fourth.

I want to know where the extracted Zolio goes and, above all, how the drug that keeps us alive reaches us. If I can follow that chain, I may reach whoever holds the complete cure. In prison I had already considered a more extreme possibility: taking a shipment of blue Zolio and using it to put pressure on the pharmaceutical company or the government. I have not shared that thought with the others. First I need to understand what I might be taking into my hands, and whether it could give me back the life I have left behind.

I return the sheet without knowing whether they asked what we want or merely wish to compare desire with obedience.

Next comes a questionnaire. I know very little, but my answers are better than they should be. The luck continues at the firing range. When they hand me a pistol, I pretend not to understand its weight or the proper stance. I fire and hit.

“Lucky me,” I say, with a laugh empty enough to serve as an excuse.

I hit with the Spencer carbine too. Khor is less fortunate: he aims, fires, and almost sends the shot towards people in the range area. An officer pulls him aside immediately with a shout.

The next test takes us to open ground. They divide us into randomly chosen pairs and make us fight. My opponent gives me no time to feign incompetence; I hit him and do enough damage for the contest to end with a clear conclusion.

Then come the maintenance tests. I repair what they put before me well, perhaps better than expected. With electronics, however, the luck runs out. The circuits resist me and leave a far less flattering result.

Obanic considers the whole as though the tests have produced an answer that does not quite fit us.

“I do not know where you came from.”

I cannot tell whether it is disappointment or surprise. I still cannot read him.

“You used to be a doctor, did you not?” he asks.

I tell him I did.

“We will continue testing you to determine where you go. I will take your file into account.”

After a week, Obanic has gathered enough observations to give us a first assessment. Mine is less impressive than that lucky first day: I am out of shape, I do not stand out in close combat, and machinery remains a field in which almost everything is beyond me. He credits me with tactical ability, some aptitude for logistics, and good aim. Above all, he thinks I am making an effort.

“Fitness can be trained. And a doctor is useful almost anywhere. I see you in the mines or in security.”

He has not written me off, though neither option is the one I ranked first. When I am allowed to revise my preferences, I leave them as they are.

In Gebedayah’s case, the good physical results need qualifying.

“You get through on brute strength. You cross the lake because you can stand in it, not because you can swim.”

The size that gets him through one test prevents him from working with many of the machines, which are too small for his hands. Obanic seems less than impressed by strength he cannot always put to use.

He reserves his harshest judgement for Khor. His physical performance is poor, but it is his attitude that makes Obanic doubt he is fit to be recruited. Khor is a poor swimmer, and in the early fights he uses his agility to dodge without trying to strike back; he has little strength and avoids exposing himself. What the supervisor objects to most, however, is his refusal to take part.

“You do not choose which tests you do here. Neither the lake nor the fights are optional.”

Afterwards I ask Khor what he makes of his assessment.

“He has it in for me.”

He is disappointed, and it is not hard to understand why. What we think of the tests matters far less than how we obey.

I find Obanic and ask how the operation is progressing. He tells me the Suelex mission is already under way and that waiting for the Council has been largely an administrative matter. He reckons we will go in a week or two, though he is disappointed by the general standard. When we gather for training again, Gebedayah and Khor are still protesting about the circuit.

“The course is not changing,” he warns them. “You still have a month left. Shape up or you will be eliminated.”

A month, now. The forecasts change faster than the orders. With no attempt to disguise his sarcasm, Khor asks what he would do if he found a cock in his bed. Obanic is not amused, and the exchange does not get us out of training.

The following weeks give us ample opportunity to separate ability from luck. My physical performance improves considerably, and I also do well enough in the security and mining tests. Logistics remains a struggle despite my interest; mechanics is still my weakest area. In one round, Gebedayah does well in logistics while Khor fails badly. In security the pattern reverses: Khor and I get good results, while Gebedayah struggles.

Khor begins to fight in earnest. He relies less and less on dodging until the test is over, and his progress becomes clear. Gebedayah seems to have stalled. We repeat the circuit, the exercises, and the assessments until our stay at Ballard has reached five weeks. Over time, Obanic becomes a little more approachable, though his manner remains cynical and distant. I know him well enough by now not to mistake every unpleasant answer for a refusal.

On the last day we visit the doctor. Wúsir is absent; we are told he will not be among the first to leave for Suelex. I take the opportunity to ask about the drug they give us: how it is stored, how much they have, how they will transport it, and where it comes from. This is the part of the move I most want to understand.

“I do not know the technical details,” the doctor replies. “But Ogelec has put good infrastructure in place.”

I get nothing specific enough to help me trace the supply chain. I must go on trusting that someone has settled the arrangements on which our survival depends.

There is no circuit that morning. They bring us out of the barracks and the supervisors begin dividing us up: six for maintenance, four for logistics. When it is the mines’ turn, they call Gebedayah and me.

“I do not want to work in the water,” Gebedayah says. He asks to be moved to security.

Obanic protests that their quota is already full and someone will have to be reassigned, but eventually agrees. I take the opportunity to ask for the same transfer.

“I can handle myself in a fight. And if people get hurt, a doctor may be more useful in security.”

He has reservations about that too, but grants it. I watch the mining group leave without us. Maintenance and logistics have already gone; those of us still with Obanic are all assigned to security.

“You are the good ones,” he tells us. “I chose you myself. I already had you in mind.”

According to him, he had places reserved for us. A moment ago there was no room; now he seems to have been expecting us. I cannot quite tell how much of a concession he has made, but our assignment is settled.

He takes us to Náster. The officers around him wear a medal and behave like soldiers before a superior. I introduce myself formally, and he receives me favourably.

“Good. A young man with the right attitude.”

I ask whether he can explain the tubes on his back. He likes the question much less than the introduction. I get no explanation, and when he shows us the map of Suelex I stop looking at his collar.

I can make out a natural canyon, scattered buildings, and a route leading to the mine. I still need to see the place before I can understand how those marks relate to one another. Náster explains that our job will be to make sure everything works.

“What should we be preparing to face?” I ask.

“Do not interrupt me.”

I wait for him to continue.

“The priority is to keep the mine running. There will be wildlife, predators, dangers. We are a unit and we will behave as one. I am the director; these are the officers. Each of you will have a commander. Obey.”

The threats are still vague, but the chain of command leaves no room for doubt. Náster has just shown us how far we can press him for explanations.

“We leave in two days. Familiarize yourselves with what lies ahead and get ready.”

I came looking for a way into the cure’s supply chain, and I will leave Ballard assigned to security. Being a doctor has helped me change roles, but I still do not know who will bring us the drug or where it will come from. I have two days before I go to guard a mine I need to understand.
