I do not enter the casino to gamble.

I arrive in the company of a bourgeois man I know. His invitation gets us
through the security checkpoint, although the police inspect us as if searching
for an excuse to detain us. I count uniforms and exits before following him to
the gaming room, where the tables, chips and gamblers present the ordinary face
of the business.

At the far end we pass through a pair of double doors into a room reserved for
the presentation. A curtained stage occupies the back wall; halfway towards it,
two performers dance around separate poles on raised side platforms while two
trapeze artists complete the show above the guests and patrons. All four wear
little enough to ensure that no one forgets what else the casino sells. I am
more interested in finding out what is being presented and who is paying for it.

Then the lights go out. The room falls silent for only a moment; then comes the
hiss of a valve from the stage and a pale glow reveals a machine of gears and
pipes, with a large cylinder at its centre.

—The moment you have all been waiting for.

The man presenting the device is corpulent. A mechanical jaw covers part of his
face and neck. This is no crude repair: its components are precise, powerful and
expensive enough that no one could mistake them for a factory mutation. It is a
display of rank.

He points at the cylinder and begins to enumerate its virtues. Blue Zolio, he
says, sublimates at ten degrees; the machine is the key component in a new
process that will allow it to be handled inside cities from its raw form. The
rest of the system disappears into technicalities, but the promise is clear
enough: less dependence on the great infrastructure beyond the cities.

  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/diary/proleg-maquina-presentacio-v8.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Colour documentary photograph of a large blue-Zolio machine on the casino’s small stage, presented by a corpulent man with an external mechanical jaw before the standing guests." loading="lazy">
</figure>

I do not know precisely which part of the process that cylinder performs or how
many more machines the system requires. Around me, however, the guests listen
as if they can already calculate the profits.

I am still watching the cylinder when I hear footsteps approaching from every
corridor at once. The doors fly open and the room collapses into its own noise.
Protesters whom I did not see enter burst in, shouting as they try to sabotage
the presentation and hurl objects towards the stage.

The presenter orders them to stop, but the objects keep striking the stage. He
draws a weapon and fires into the air. The shot cuts through the shouting for
an instant; then he lowers the barrel and fires at the protesters.

Above the screams I hear a continuous whistle. Whitish smoke spills from the
installation and pours off the stage. Everyone knows what it means: there is a
Zolio leak. The gunfire destroys any hope of order, but it is the gas that turns
the room into a stampede.

The crowd surges without knowing where to go and tears apart the groups that
filled the hall. I run with the others. In that moment there are no guests,
patrons, protesters or priests; only bodies seeking an exit before they are
trampled, shot or forced to breathe more gas.

When we manage to get outside, our escape ends at the door. Copper Square is
wide enough for nearly five hundred people to spread across it and bare enough
that no one can find shelter. It is circular, without a single tree. A hundred
police officers have formed a semicircle in front of the casino, cutting us off
from the rest of the square. The street lamps, advertising columns and copper
shop signs seem to trace the edges of the trap; among the exposed pipes, small
drinking fountains continue to flow as if this were any other day.

A man with a feather on his head climbs a ladder and raises a megaphone. He has
enough access and presence for me to take him for someone important, but I do
not know whether he commands the police or the casino. His voice descends,
warped by the metal, as he tries to clear a path for the wealthy.

The crowd revolts before he can manage it, and that is when four lorries arrive.
They unload almost a hundred standard civil-control units: light, rounded
automatons with a ball for a head, a smiling face and a propeller that allows
them to fly. The truncheon in one hand and pincer in the other belie the
friendliness of the design. One comes straight at me and seizes me before I can
move aside.

I give it no reason to tighten its grip. I surrender at once and pray.

—Please, remain still —the megaphone repeats, but the square keeps shouting and
struggling until a second convoy of five lorries appears.

About fifty heavy automatons roll out: humanoid figures on triangular tracks,
armed with rotary machine guns. Their arrival says more about the situation
than any order from the megaphone. They need not fire. The mouths of their guns
are eloquent enough to freeze the panic until, little by little, the square
falls still.

The police take advantage of the silence. The officers tighten the cordon while
the light units herd us together and the Gatlings watch the four approaches.
Before long, what had been a crowd becomes rows of detainees. When they set us
moving, no one needs to announce our destination.

They march us to the prison through streets that have already been evacuated.
There are no onlookers at the windows or pedestrians stranded at the crossings;
only us, the guards and the regular noise of the machines escorting us. As we
advance, the clean-up is already beginning behind us.

The first blue stains appear beside the lips of a man walking a few paces ahead
of me. Soon afterwards I recognise the same colour around a woman’s eyes. I know
what it means before the others begin touching their faces: we have been
exposed to Zolio and are still within the contagious period. The empty streets
and clean-up no longer look like a response to the unrest; they know what they
are transporting.

When we arrive, they are already removing the prisoners from one of the blocks.
They are not merely preparing a cell for us. They are preparing a quarantine.
